# AI Consultant Cost UK: Day Rates, Fees and What You Get

_What an AI consultant costs in the UK: independent contract day rates and permanent salaries, what you actually get for the money, and when hiring one is the wrong move._

AI consultant cost in the UK runs roughly 440 to 734 GBP a day for a contractor, with a median of 575 GBP, based on job adverts in the six months to 23 November 2026. A permanent AI consultant earns a median of 80,000 GBP a year. Consultancy firms charge more than contractor rates, and a short fixed-scope engagement is often the cheapest way to get a decision you can act on.

Those figures come from IT Jobs Watch, which tracks UK job adverts (contract day rates and permanent salaries), so they are what employers and agencies advertise to pay individual consultants. They are the best independent benchmark we could find. They are not what a consultancy will invoice you, and this guide explains the gap, what you actually get for the money and when hiring a consultant is the wrong move.

## Why are UK businesses paying for AI consultants?

Most UK firms that want to use AI get stuck on the same three things: knowing where to start, the cost, and a lack of expertise inside the team. A consultant is one way to buy those answers quickly.

The Office for National Statistics found in its July 2026 review of AI in UK businesses that around 35% of businesses with 10 or more employees now use at least one AI technology, up from around 12% in late 2023. But only 10% of those users report using AI extensively. Adoption is wide and shallow: plenty of firms have tried a chatbot, far fewer have changed how work gets done.

ONS's earlier analysis found that the most common factors holding firms back were difficulty identifying business use cases, cost and a lack of expertise. The expertise gap is sharpest in exactly the firms most likely to read this guide: around 18% of businesses with 100 to 249 employees said insufficient expertise had delayed their adoption. Those three barriers are, more or less, the job description of an AI consultant. The question is whether you pay for them by the day, by the deliverable or by hiring someone.

When it works, the return can be real. In a Gartner survey of 822 business leaders, conducted between September and November 2023, early adopters of generative AI reported a 15.8% revenue increase, 15.2% cost savings and 22.6% productivity improvement on average. Gartner was careful to add that these benefits are very company and use case specific, which is exactly why the first job of any consultant should be to find your specific case.

## How much does an AI consultant cost in the UK?

A contract AI consultant costs a median of 575 GBP a day in the UK, and a permanent one earns a median of 80,000 GBP a year. Those are advertised rates for individuals; firms add overheads and margin on top.

Here is the spread, from IT Jobs Watch contract and permanent data for the six months to 23 November 2026:

| Measure | Contract (daily rate) | Permanent (annual salary) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10th percentile | 440 GBP | 60,000 GBP |
| Median | 575 GBP | 80,000 GBP |
| 90th percentile | 734 GBP | 98,750 GBP |
| Rates or salaries quoted | 67 | 172 |

Two things are worth knowing about these numbers. First, the samples are small: 67 daily rates and 172 salaries, because "AI consultant" is still a young job title. Treat them as a guide, not a price list. Second, location matters less than you might expect. The median daily rate outside London was 564 GBP, close to the national figure, and the permanent median outside London was 82,500 GBP, slightly above it.

To turn a day rate into a budget, multiply it by the days. A ten-day engagement at the median rate is 5,750 GBP before VAT; at the 90th percentile it is 7,340 GBP. That is the cost of an individual contractor. A consultancy firm will usually quote more per day, because it carries sales, management, insurance and margin, and because you may get a team rather than one person. We have not found a reliable public dataset of UK consultancy day rates, so we will not invent one; ask each firm for its day rate, the number of days and the names of the people who will do the work.

## What does an AI consultant actually do?

A good AI consultant finds the few places where AI will pay back in your business, tells you whether to build, buy or leave each one, and hands you a plan with costs attached. A weak one gives you a deck about AI in general.

KORIX defines a useful AI consulting engagement as one that ends with four things you can act on: a ranked list of use cases, a build-or-buy call on each, a governance plan and a costed roadmap. In practice, the work covers each of them:

- **Use case discovery:** mapping where your team spends time on repeatable work, and ranking the candidates by value and feasibility.
- **Build or buy:** for each candidate, an honest call on whether an existing tool already does it, whether it needs building, or whether it is not worth doing yet.
- **Risk and governance:** which data can be used, where a person must check the output, and what your regulator or clients will expect to see.
- **A costed roadmap:** what to do first, in what order, and what each step will cost.

Be sceptical of anyone who claims to already know the answer for your business. Ethan Mollick, the Wharton professor who writes the One Useful Thing newsletter, put it bluntly in May 2025: "Nobody has special information about how to best use AI at your company, or a playbook for how to integrate it into your organization." A consultant's real value is a disciplined method for finding out quickly, plus the experience to spot what will not work, not a secret playbook.

## What makes an AI consultant more or less expensive?

Four things move the price most: how much of the work needs specialist technical depth, how regulated your sector is, how messy your data and systems are, and whether you are buying advice or a working system. Location and job title matter far less.

- **Depth of expertise:** a consultant who can design and build an AI workflow, not just recommend one, sits towards the top of the range. The IT Jobs Watch 75th percentile daily rate was 675 GBP, a long way above the 440 GBP at the 10th.
- **Regulation:** if you are regulated by the FCA, the SRA or a similar body, the work has to cover data handling, human review and audit trails. That adds days, and it should, because it is where a careless recommendation does the most damage.
- **Data and systems:** AI is only as useful as the information it can reach. If your client records live across a practice management system, spreadsheets and email, expect more time spent on mapping and cleaning before anything useful can be recommended.
- **Advice or delivery:** a strategy engagement ends with a plan. An implementation ends with a running system. The second costs more, but it is the only one that changes how your team works on Monday.

Size of firm matters less than people assume. A 30-person accountancy practice with one painful, well-defined process can be cheaper to help than a 150-person firm that wants "an AI strategy" without a starting point. The narrower and better described the problem, the lower the bill.

## Day rate, fixed fee or retainer: which pricing model is better?

For a first engagement, a fixed fee with a defined deliverable is usually better than a day rate, because it puts the risk of overrun on the consultant. Day rates suit open-ended work, and retainers suit ongoing support once a system is running.

- **Day rate:** you pay for time. It is flexible, but the total is unknown until the end, and nobody is rewarded for finishing early.
- **Fixed fee:** you pay for a defined output, such as a costed roadmap in a set number of days. You know the cost upfront; the trade-off is a tighter scope.
- **Retainer:** a monthly fee for a set amount of support. Useful after launch, when you need someone to tune and maintain what has been built, but poor value if nothing is running yet.

Whatever the model, insist on knowing what you will hold at the end. Gartner's warning on generative AI projects applies here: it predicted that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs or unclear business value. "Unfortunately, there is no one size fits all with GenAI, and costs aren't as predictable as other technologies," said Rita Sallam, Distinguished VP Analyst at Gartner. An engagement that ends without a clear go or no-go has added to that cost without reducing the uncertainty.

Keep the scale in proportion, too. Gartner put the cost of the most ambitious generative AI deployments, the ones that change a business model, at 5 million to 20 million USD. Those are enterprise programmes. A UK service firm of 20 to 150 people should not be pricing its first step against them: the right first engagement is measured in days and thousands, not months and millions.

## Is a freelance AI consultant cheaper than a firm or an in-house hire?

A freelancer is usually cheapest per day, a firm costs more but brings cover and breadth, and an in-house hire only makes sense when you have enough AI work to keep someone busy all year.

At the median permanent salary of 80,000 GBP, a full-time AI consultant on your payroll costs more than the salary once you add employer National Insurance, pension, equipment and recruitment. That is good value if you have a steady pipeline of AI projects. It is poor value if you need one decision and one system, because you are paying for a year to solve a problem that might take a month.

A freelancer at the median day rate is the cheapest route to a specific piece of work, but you depend on one person's availability and judgement. A firm costs more per day, and you should check who will actually do the work rather than who pitched it. ## Does IR35 matter if you hire a contractor AI consultant?

It can. If you engage an individual consultant through their own limited company, the off-payroll working rules, known as IR35, may apply, and the rules differ for small businesses and for mid-sized or large ones.

HMRC's guidance on understanding off-payroll working explains that the rules apply when a worker providing services through their own intermediary would have been an employee if they had worked for you directly. If your firm is mid-sized or large, you carry more of the responsibility for deciding that status. Engaging a consultancy that supplies a defined deliverable, rather than a person working under your direction, usually changes the picture, but check with your accountant before you sign. This is not tax advice.

## When should you not hire an AI consultant?

Do not hire one if you cannot name a process that costs you real time or money, if nobody internally will own the result, or if you already know what you want built. In each case the consulting fee buys you little.

- **No specific problem yet:** if the brief is "we should be doing something with AI", spend an afternoon listing the repeatable tasks that eat your team's week first. Our guide to seven signs your business is ready for AI is a free place to start.
- **No owner:** a roadmap nobody is accountable for becomes a document in a drawer.
- **You already know the answer:** if you can describe the workflow and the outcome, skip the strategy work and go straight to scoping a build.
- **The tool already exists:** if an off-the-shelf product does the job, you need a licence and some configuration, not a consultant.

## What questions should you ask before you sign?

Ask what you will hold at the end, who will do the work, and how the consultant makes money after the engagement. Those three answers reveal most of what a proposal hides.

- **What exactly will I have at the end?** Look for named artefacts: a ranked use case list, a build-or-buy recommendation, a governance plan and a costed roadmap.
- **Who will do the work, and for how many days?** Day rate x days x named people is the only fair way to compare quotes.
- **What do you earn if I follow your advice?** A consultant who also sells software licences or a build has a reason to recommend them. That is not automatically wrong, but you should know.
- **Who owns the outputs?** You should keep every document, model and framework you paid for.
- **Can I speak to a client you have done this for?** Ask for one with a published review you can check independently.

Here is what that looks like in practice. Imagine three quotes for the same brief. The first is a day rate with "approximately two to four weeks of discovery". The second is a fixed price for a strategy report, with no mention of who writes it. The third is a fixed price for a named consultant to deliver a ranked use case list, a build-or-buy call and a costed roadmap in one week. The first looks cheapest per day and is the least predictable. The second is predictable but vague about what you will be able to act on. The third is the only one where you can check, at the end, whether you got what you paid for. Price matters, but it should be the last thing you compare, not the first.
